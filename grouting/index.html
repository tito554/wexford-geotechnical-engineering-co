Grouting Design for Wexford’s Variable Subsoil: What We’ve Learned on Site

Together, we solve the challenges of tomorrow.

READ MORE →

The mix of soils under Wexford town doesn’t always announce itself until you’re three metres down. One borehole hits dense stony till left by the last glaciation; fifty metres away, you’re in soft alluvial silt from the Slaney estuary that barely holds a trench wall. That contrast is exactly why grouting design here takes more than a textbook approach. In our experience, a single grouting method rarely covers a full site: we often pair low-pressure permeation grouting in the gravel lenses with jet grouting where the silt gets deep, and we lean on CPT testing to map the transition zones before specifying injection parameters. Where retaining structures abut tidal mud, the design also has to account for chlorides attacking cement durability, a detail we’ve seen overlooked in more generic specs brought from inland projects.

Grouting design in Wexford is essentially about respecting the estuary: it dictates where the grout goes, how fast it sets, and how long the treated ground will last.

Process overview

The grouting rigs we mobilise around Wexford tend to be compact track-mounted units that can work inside existing buildings—old warehouses along the quay, for instance—without taking down walls. A typical setup includes a colloidal mixer, a high-pressure triplex pump, and a real-time data logger that records flow rate, pressure and volume take per stage. When we run jet grouting, the monitor displays grout pressure up to 40 MPa and rotation speed, because in the silty layers near Crescent Quay you need that energy to erode and mix the soil into a column that will bear. What makes the difference locally is the packer spacing: in the till, we can grout longer stages, but in the laminated silts we shorten the stage length and tighten the refusal criteria, otherwise the grout travels laterally into the estuary mud and you lose control of the bulb geometry. This kind of adaptive parameter setting is where in-situ permeability testing before grouting proves its worth, giving us a real Lugeon value to calibrate injection pressure and mix viscosity.

Local context

Wexford’s glacial and post-glacial deposits sit on Cambrian metasediments, but the real risk for grouting lies in the buried channels filled with soft estuarine clays that reach depths of 15 metres near the harbour. Groundwater here is tidally influenced: the Slaney’s level fluctuates two metres between low and high water, and that pulse moves through the granular layers, washing out fresh grout if you inject at the wrong phase of the tide. We’ve also hit layers where the silt contains enough organic content to delay cement hydration, so the design has to either use accelerator admixtures or increase the cement factor. Ignoring these local effects can leave a grout curtain with unsealed windows, and in a town where basement excavations are increasingly common for mixed-use developments along the riverside, a failed curtain means pumping costs that can kill a construction budget.

Additional services

01

Permeation and compensation grouting

Low-pressure injection of microfine cements or chemical grouts into granular soils to reduce permeability beneath foundations or ahead of tunnel faces. We design the grid pattern, stage length and refusal criteria based on Lugeon values from site tests.

02

Jet grouting for underpinning and cut-off

High-energy soil mixing to form soilcrete columns or panels, particularly in the soft silts along the Slaney. We specify single, double or triple fluid systems depending on the required column diameter and the presence of organics.

03

Grout curtain verification and monitoring

Post-grouting permeability testing, core drilling through treated ground, and real-time monitoring of groundwater levels behind curtains. We tie the verification directly to the design assumptions so there are no surprises during excavation.

Quick answers

How much does a grouting design package cost for a typical Wexford project?

For a standalone grouting design—including site investigation review, injection parameter specification, method statement, and verification plan—fees typically range from €1.300 to €3.790, depending on the size of the treatment zone, the number of grout types required, and whether the project is near the estuary where tidal groundwater complicates the design.

Which grouting method works best in Wexford’s glacial till?

In the dense stony till, permeation grouting with microfine cement is usually the most predictable, because the till has a matrix of fines that limits travel distance. We run Lugeon tests first to confirm permeability and then design the stage length and injection pressure accordingly. Where the till is interspersed with gravel lenses, jet grouting may be needed to create homogeneous columns.

How do you verify that a grout curtain is watertight before excavation?

We drill check boreholes through the curtain and run Lugeon or falling-head permeability tests, comparing the results against the design target (typically ≤ 1 × 10⁻⁶ m/s for cut-off curtains). Core recovery also shows us the continuity of grout filling. In Wexford’s tidally influenced groundwater, we monitor piezometer levels over at least one full tidal cycle to confirm the curtain has cut off the hydraulic connection.

Does the Slaney estuary affect grout durability?

Yes, the brackish water and sulphate-bearing soils near the estuary can attack ordinary Portland cement over time. Our designs specify sulphate-resistant cement or microfine blends with proven durability in chloride environments, and we reference IS EN 206 exposure classes XS and XA when setting the mix design. In some cases we add silica fume to reduce permeability of the hardened grout.
